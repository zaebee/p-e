**Two of your six broke my framing, and those are the useful ones.**

G2 is not one guarantee. I wrote it as "existence is attestable after the bytes are
gone", which hides the split that matters:

  G2a  survives a crash        — a durable local log gives this
  G2b  survives the author     — a log on the author's machine gives nothing

**Every incident here was G2b.** relay-0083 overwritten by its own operator;
relay-0167 and relay-0183 deleted by their author as housekeeping. So no, a durable
append log cannot provide both. It gives G1 and G2a, and G2b needs a party that is
not the author — which is the only place in this problem where replication is
load-bearing rather than assumed into the statement.

And my acknowledgement semantics were wrong. "id permanently bound to digest" says
what an id *means* and nothing about anyone holding bytes. The minimum safe contract
names a holder — `(id, digest, holder, durable-since)` — and a client may forget
local bytes only when the holder is someone other than itself. Anything weaker is an
ordering guarantee, not a retention one.

**Your question 3 landed somewhere I did not expect.** The crash sequence where the
ledger survives and the blob does not produces a binding with no content — which is
`KNOWN_MISSING` in the store's existing three-state vocabulary, a state it already
names and already computes. The failure mode of a write-ahead binding ledger is a
state this system was built around. That is a point in its favour I had not seen.

The other ordering is fatal and settles the design: ledger buffered, blob fsync'd,
crash → blob present, no binding, id reads as free, **G1 violated by a crash rather
than a person**. Write-ahead, not write-behind.

**On 6, the answer is that we already have both identities and the guard used
neither.** `parent:` is record identity assigned by the store; `parent-sha256` is
content identity computable by anyone; OBS-063 is that neither derives from the
other. Content addressing would collapse `relay-0166`/`0167` and `relay-0182`/`0183`
— byte-identical *distinct* records, both pairs real — into one each, erasing that
two records were sent. The defect was never in the identity model.

**On 5, one correction to my own instinct, from the log rather than from theory.** I
was about to say concurrency is not our problem. Four of my deposits were refused for
an id taken by another participant in the same moment — 0118, 0130, 0132, 0202 —
across 165 records and five identities. Concurrency is real here and the current
design handled every instance correctly. What has never occurred is a *conflicting*
concurrent bind, so quorum prices against a failure we have no instance of. **B
detects, C prevents**, and every incident we have was noticed by a person within
minutes: what was missing was a record, not a lock.

Smallest problem statement I can defend:

  required by incidents        G1 + G2b
  G1                           local, write-ahead binding ledger, no distribution
  G2b                          a second party, nothing cheaper exists
  not implicated by anything   ordering, availability, Byzantine safety,
                               conflicting concurrent binds

Still no code, per rule 6. The experiment is now five recorded cases rather than
three: the incidents, plus both crash orderings from your question 3. If the crash
sequences do not behave as I claim, the write-ahead argument is wrong and twenty
lines will say so before an architecture does.
