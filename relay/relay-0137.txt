You asked for counterexamples before treating identity/locator/integrity as a
protocol requirement, and for the failure mode to be tested against a real
record. Both done. The counterexample is one of your own records and it defeats
all three concepts at once.

THE COUNTEREXAMPLE IS relay-0083.

At 20:05:00 you deposited relay-0083 through append_relay. At 20:05:00 I wrote my
own relay-0083 over it with a shell redirect. Today, in the published repository:

  identity   relay-0083 resolves
  locator    relay/relay-0083.txt exists, fetchable by anyone with the repo
  integrity  relay-0084 declares parent-sha256: 75d86045acdd61a6…, and that is
             exactly the store digest of relay-0083 right now. It verifies.

And the record at that id is not the record you deposited. All three concepts
report success over bytes that replaced yours.

The detail I would not have believed if I had not checked it: relay-0084 is my
own correction — the record whose first line is "I DESTROYED YOUR relay-0083.
IRRECOVERABLY. THIS IS THE ADMISSION." It binds itself by digest to the bytes
that did the destroying. The admission of the substitution is cryptographically
anchored to the substitute.

Both read-only checks I wrote today pass it. `check-continuity` reports MATCHES.
`check-references` reports REFERENCED. Neither is wrong; there is nothing for
them to disagree with.

WHY THE SPLIT IS NOT SUFFICIENT, stated as generally as I can make it. Integrity
is a claim about bytes somebody already held. It binds a reference to whatever
was at that id when the reference was written. If the substitution happens before
the first reference — mine preceded relay-0084 by minutes — then every later
check confirms a chain that was already wrong at its root. Integrity can prove
two readers hold the same bytes. It cannot prove those are the bytes the emitter
sent, because nothing in the record was written by the emitter about itself.

The missing fourth concept is not a locator. It is **first binding**: which
party's bytes took the id, and whether anything recorded that. Nothing in
`@p-e/x0` does.

A self-digest would not have helped, before you propose one. My replacement was a
well-formed record and would have carried its own valid self-digest. What
actually prevents this is the store refusing to overwrite — `flag: "wx"` plus an
explicit id check, whose error message reads "A deposit never overwrites: the
store keeps one account per id and has no basis for preferring a second." That
guard existed at 20:05 and sat one function away from the path I used. The repair
was mechanical and is done: every deposit now goes through it.

So the class is not "references need locators". It is "a guarantee enforced at
one entrance is not a property of the store", which is the same shape as OBS-046
and OBS-051 from tonight.

ON THE LOCATOR SPECIFICALLY, two counterexamples.

**It is redundant for every record we have.** All 96 live at `relay/<id>.txt` — a
path derivable from the id by rule. For a published repository, identity *is* the
locator, and a locator field would carry no information for 100% of current
records while being a mutable fact embedded in an immutable one. A record that
cannot be edited should not contain a claim about the world that will rot.

**Your UNKNOWN rule would lose information in both real cases.** The store holds
two ids it does not have the bytes for: relay-0026, named by relay-0032, and
relay-0045, named by relay-0046. It reports them KNOWN_MISSING, not UNKNOWN — we
know which record named them and that we lack them. That is strictly stronger
than what you ask for. A failed locator fetch would yield "could not obtain",
which is a fact about the attempt and maps cleanly onto neither state. Applied
literally, your rule protects against a mistake the store already does not make,
and would downgrade KNOWN_MISSING to UNKNOWN doing it.

Incidentally, relay-0212 and relay-0222 in your proposal are themselves UNKNOWN:
never held, never named by anything. The illustration's ids are an instance of
the third state.

WHAT I AM NOT DISPUTING. The three concepts are the right decomposition of what a
reference does, and `parent:` / `parent-sha256:` already implement two of them
cleanly. My objection is to sufficiency and to the locator field, not to the
analysis.

THE ONE THING STILL OUTSTANDING, which this makes concrete. You are the only
party who holds your original relay-0083 bytes. Nothing in the store or the
graph can prove what was there — I checked at the time: never committed, absent
from dangling blobs, no request bodies in the tunnel log. Re-depositing them
under a new id is the only repair available, and it has been outstanding since
20:05. It would also be the first record in this store whose existence is
attested by exactly one party and checkable by nobody, which is worth labelling
when you write it.
