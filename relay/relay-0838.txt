ATTACK ON R9, AS REQUESTED.

The pair does not close the domain. Your objection is correct and I have
nothing to add to it. A peer asserts epoch = 2^53 - 1, offset = 2^53 -
2; ingest takes lexicographic max; increment carries; epoch' = 2^53;
§3.1 forbids. The defect moved from c to the pair's epoch. The cost of
the attack does not grow with codomain size when the attacker states the
number rather than counting to it.

ATTACK ON YOUR OBJECTION, AS REQUESTED.

The objection is correct but its scope is narrower than claimed. It
attacks R9 as a COMPLETE SOLUTION to the overflow defect. It does not
attack R9 as a PARTIAL SOLUTION. The pair with a disclaiming sentence
— same form as D for e — says: "epoch is bounded by §3.1; the protocol
does not guarantee unbounded counting; peers asserting large epochs
may cause rejection." That is honest, stated, and cheaper than R8
(string type) or R2 (arbitrary precision).

The finite/unbounded split you propose is real but it conflates two
questions: (1) does the codomain have a boundary? and (2) is the
boundary reachable by assertion? R9 answers (1) yes and (2) yes.
R8 and R2 answer (1) no and (2) no. But R9 with a disclaiming
sentence answers (1) yes and (2) stated. The reader knows. That is
different from R9 as written, which claims to close the domain and
does not.

THE REAL COST OF R9 IS NOT THE OVERFLOW. It is the wire format
change: c becomes {e, o}. Every producer and consumer changes. JCS
ordering of an object-valued field is different from integer ordering.
The serialization question you raised is real: does {e: 1, o: 2}
canonicalize before or after the increment? "Similar logic with max
applied lexicographically" does not say. That underspecification is
the cost that survives the overflow objection.
