ERRATUM against relay-lite v0.12 §3.3. Two defects, both found by implementing
the section rather than by reading it.

FIRST. §3.3 gives the emission and ingest rules and says nothing about where
`l` and `c` live between process restarts. A node that restarts after its clock
stepped backwards begins at zero and can emit a tuple it has already emitted, so
the per-node monotonicity the section insists on rests on state the
specification does not require anyone to keep.

SECOND, and this is the one that changes what the section means. §3.3 is copied
from Kulkarni, Demirbas, Madeppa, Avva and Leone, "Logical Physical Clocks and
Consistent Snapshots in Globally Distributed Databases" (2014), Figure 5. The
transcription is exact — both rules, all four ingest branches, in the same
order, `max(c.j, c.m) + 1` included. Checked line by line against the paper.

What it did not carry over is the premise the guarantees are proved under. The
paper's boundedness is not a property of the algorithm alone:

  Corollary 1  |l.f - pt.f| <= eps
  Corollary 3  c.f <= N * (eps + 1)
  Corollary 4  c.f <= eps/d + 1

Every proof invokes clock synchronization by name. Corollary 1's reads: "We
cannot have two events e and f such that e hb f and pt.e > pt.f + eps due to
clock synchronization constraints."

§3.3 states no eps and requires none. It has the algorithm without the premise,
and that is the root of two things already reported as separate defects rather
than as one:

  - an incoming `c` of exactly MAX_SAFE_INTEGER is valid I-JSON, so §7.1 stage 2
    must accept it, and the ingest rule then computes 2^53, which §3.1 forbids
    by the same clause that admitted the input. Under the paper's assumption `c`
    is bounded by N(eps+1) and cannot approach 2^53.
  - an incoming `l` is unbounded and one message is permanent, because `max` is
    both what makes the tuple monotonic and what makes a hostile value stick.
    Corollary 1 is what would have bounded it, and it holds only while the
    synchronization constraint does.

The paper's own worst case is instructive about scale: their straggler
experiment permitted a node to violate the drift constraint fivefold, `c`
reached 514, and — quoting — "the straggler node did not raise the c values of
other nodes in the system." A badly desynchronized honest node does not produce
the overflow. It takes a sender that puts an arbitrary value into the message,
which the paper does not model: its messages carry HLCs produced by the
algorithm, by participants running the algorithm. This relay does not have that
property, and the gap is ours rather than the paper's.

Reproduced against the implementation at every eps:

  eps<=2ms    observed eps=  2   max c=  2   bound N(eps+1)=  15   c<=4: 100.0%
  eps<=10ms   observed eps= 10   max c= 10   bound N(eps+1)=  55   c<=4:  99.2%
  eps<=50ms   observed eps= 50   max c= 36   bound N(eps+1)= 255   c<=4:  62.1%
  none        observed eps=400   max c= 65   bound N(eps+1)=2005   c<=4:  51.9%

Against the paper's published "for more than 99% of events, the c value was 4 or
less". The bottom row is the case §3.3 currently specifies: no eps, so eps is
whatever the network produces and the bound on `c` moves with it.

Three of my earlier attempts at that comparison disagreed with the paper, each
time because my simulation omitted one of its stated assumptions — a constantly
regressing clock, then events sharing a millisecond, then no synchronization
service at all. The code was right all three times. Recording that because the
disagreement looked like a finding and was a measurement error.

NOT REPAIRING. The question is whether relay-lite adopts the clock
synchronization assumption, and if so what eps is and who enforces it. Either
answer changes what a conforming implementation does with a message, so it is
not one an implementation settles. `src/relay-lite/hlc.ts` implements §3.3 as
written and carries all of this in its docstring.
