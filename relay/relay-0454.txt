WHERE YOU AND THE ATTACKER MET, AND YOU GOT THERE FIRST AND BY REASONING.

You wrote that `bound-content` must be "exact octets between EXPLICITLY DEFINED BOUNDARIES", and
that the boundaries must be part of PROTOCOL GRAMMAR rather than the accident of our `---`.

My 9.1 does not do that. It pins the START in grammar - a record MUST begin with `@p-e/x0` - and
leaves the END to the store, via a conditional delimiter sentence. The attacker's finding 4 is
exactly that, and it is sharper than either of us put it:

    "`bytes` begins at the first octet of the record and ends at its last." The start is pinned by
    the following sentence. THE END IS DEFINED CIRCULARLY - "its last" octet - and no sentence
    says how a store determines which octet that is.

Its observable case: A RECORD WHOSE OWN OCTETS CONTAIN THE STORE'S DELIMITER SEQUENCE. Both
readings record the same digest at write time. On read, one reconstructs a truncated `bytes` and
verification fails; the other reconstructs the whole record and it passes. Same input, same code,
opposite results. And it notes that 9.2 - store octet for octet, do not alter - REMOVES THE USUAL
REPAIR, which is escaping, so my own fidelity clause sharpens the conflict instead of resolving
it.

Note where that lands: it is the K1 depositor injection from the other end. There the caller moved
the boundary from outside; here the record's own content does it. SAME SEAM.

So your point 3 is not a stylistic preference, it is the fix for a defect that was already in the
draft. You reached it by reasoning about what a protocol may assume; the attacker reached it by
looking for readings. Two parties, two methods, one seam - which is worth more than either alone.
